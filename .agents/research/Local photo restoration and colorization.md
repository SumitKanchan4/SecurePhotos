# Restore old photos locally, without a GPU

SecurePhotos can offer good offline restoration and colourisation on an ordinary laptop CPU if it uses compact CNN models and avoids diffusion entirely. The recommended stack has five pieces. **Real-ESRGAN general-x4v3** handles upscaling and mild denoising and is already shipped (BSD-3, 4.9 MB, **23 s measured** for a 1500×1000 scan on the owner's Ryzen 7 5700U). **The BOPBTL scratch detector plus Big-LaMa** handle damage repair (MIT / Apache-2.0, 2.5 s and 3.7 s per 512 px tile measured). **GFPGAN v1.4** restores faces (Apache-2.0 code and weights, 340 MB, about 1–3 s per face *estimated*). **DDColor** colourises (Apache-2.0 code and weights, **0.5–3.2 s measured**). **SCUNet** and **FBCNN** are optional heavy cleanup for very grainy or JPEG sources. A typical "Enhance" therefore finishes in about 30 s, and a full repair + faces + colour pass in one to two minutes, all on CPU. Diffusion restorers (SUPIR, DiffBIR, OSDFace, StableSR, CtrlColor) are not viable on CPU, and most are non-commercial anyway. Licensing has one unresolved weak point: every usable face model was trained on FFHQ (CC BY-NC-SA) and every colouriser on ImageNet (non-commercial research terms). Whether those dataset terms reach the trained weights is unsettled law. That is a residual risk to document, not a blocker, but CodeFormer, GPEN, SUPIR, MAT and the other explicitly non-commercial models must stay out. The product rules follow from how these models fail. They invent detail, so restoration must be on demand. It must save a new version and keep the original. It needs a before/after view, strength and per-face controls, and an identity check. Every output that reconstructs faces or adds colour must carry an "AI" label in the app and IPTC `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` metadata in exported files, which also covers the EU AI Act Article 50 marking duty that now applies.

## A deterministic CNN chain fits a laptop CPU; diffusion does not

The target hardware sets the shape of the answer. Research benchmarks for this report ran ONNX Runtime 1.29 CPU EP on the owner's **Ryzen 7 5700U (8 cores / 16 threads, Zen 2, 15 GB RAM, no dGPU)**, using random tensors because timing does not depend on image content. Those numbers are marked *measured* below. Everything else is an estimate from parameter counts and must still be checked on real photos.

| Model (ONNX, fp32) | File | Measured on 5700U | Peak RAM | Est. for a 1500×1000 scan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real-ESRGAN general-x4v3 | 4.9 MB | 23.3 s, all 24 tiles to 6000×4000 | ~220 MB (+288 MB output buffer) | ~23 s |
| RRDB-body 1x model (x4plus-class) | 66.9 MB | 339 s, 24 tiles | ~670 MB | ~5.6 min |
| BOPBTL scratch detector | 150.6 MB | 2.5 s per 512² | ~1.1 GB | 3–15 s on a downscaled copy |
| Big-LaMa inpainting | 207.6 MB | 3.7 s per 512² crop | ~1.3 GB | 4–45 s (damaged crops only) |
| SCUNet real-PSNR denoise | 74.4 MB | 8.5 s per 512² tile | **~5.7 GB** | ~100 s |
| FBCNN JPEG cleanup | 287.7 MB | 6.0 s per 512² tile | ~1.2 GB | ~72 s |

**Damage repair** works on CPU as a two-step pipeline. First the Microsoft "Bringing Old Photos Back to Life" scratch detector (repo **MIT**, weights released as GitHub assets with no separate licence) finds scratches. Then Big-LaMa (code **Apache-2.0**, trained on Places) inpaints only the 512 px crops that contain damage ([BOPBTL](https://github.com/microsoft/Bringing-Old-Photos-Back-to-Life), [LaMa](https://github.com/advimman/lama)). Community ONNX exports of both exist, with per-file licence notices and PyTorch parity checks ([artiva-ai/modelos](https://huggingface.co/artiva-ai/modelos)). Two cautions apply. Microsoft never released its labelled scratch dataset, and it warns the 256 px-trained model "may not work ideally for arbitrary resolution", so the detector will miss scratches or flag paper texture. The app needs a **user-editable mask (brush and eraser)** before LaMa runs. Also, LaMa rebuilds texture, not faces, so a crease through a face has to go to the face stage. The obvious alternative, MAT, is **CC BY-NC 4.0** and so not usable ([MAT](https://github.com/fenglinglwb/MAT)). BOPBTL's own global restoration network is dated and unmaintained, and only its detector is worth keeping.

**Denoise and JPEG cleanup** should usually come free with the upscale. General-x4v3 has a denoise-strength knob (`-dn`, 0–1), though it needs the companion `wdn` weights, and its authors call its deblur and denoise "not too strong" ([Real-ESRGAN model zoo](https://www.Github.com/xinntao/Real-ESRGAN/blob/master/docs/model_zoo.md)). For very grainy scans, **SCUNet real-PSNR** (Apache-2.0, [SCUNet](https://github.com/cszn/SCUNet)) is the strongest permissive blind denoiser, and DiffBIR itself uses it as its stage 1 ([DiffBIR](https://github.com/XPixelGroup/DiffBIR)). Its measured 5.7 GB peak would crash 8 GB laptops unless tiles shrink and the memory arena is constrained. **FBCNN** (Apache-2.0) is worth running only when the source really is a JPEG, such as a phone snap of a print. NAFNet and Restormer are MIT, but they were trained on smartphone sensor noise (SIDD) and GoPro motion blur, which do not match film grain, paper texture or halftone ([NAFNet](https://github.com/megvii-research/NAFNet), [Restormer](https://github.com/swz30/Restormer)). OpenModelDB's 1x RealPLKSR denoisers are CC-BY-4.0 (credit required) and need ONNX conversion and benchmarking first ([1x DeNoise realplksr](https://openmodeldb.info/models/1x-DeNoise-realplksr-otf)). Avoid 1x JPEGDestroyer, which is GPL-3.0.

**Upscaling** should stay on compact architectures. The RRDB body that Real-ESRGAN x4plus uses measured **about 15× slower** than general-x4v3 (339 s vs 23 s), and SwinIR, HAT and DAT transformers cost more still and export fragile dynamic-shape ONNX ([Heliosoph/swinir-onnx](https://huggingface.co/Heliosoph/swinir-onnx)). The best "better quality at similar cost" candidate is a SPAN-class photo model such as **4x Nomos8k SPAN otf medium (CC-BY-4.0)** ([OpenModelDB](https://openmodeldb.info/models/4x-Nomos8k-span-otf-medium)), though its CPU speed is an unmeasured estimate. Old prints rarely need 4×, so default to a 2× output and offer 4× as an option.

Diffusion restorers fail on both counts. SUPIR's low-VRAM mode still needs "12G for Diffusion, 16G for LLaVA" ([SUPIR](https://fr.github.com/Fanghua-Yu/SUPIR)), and its weights are non-commercial, defining even "process[ing] images that are intended for sale" as commercial use ([SUPIR LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Fanghua-Yu/SUPIR/master/LICENSE)). HYPIR uses the same SupPixel terms, while StableSR and InvSR are S-Lab non-commercial ([StableSR](https://github.com/IceClear/StableSR), [InvSR](https://github.com/zsyOAOA/InvSR)). One-step OSEDiff still runs an SD 2.1 UNet, so on a laptop CPU that means minutes per scan (estimate). Even the commercial reference point, Topaz's local Dust & Scratch v2, wants 8 GB of VRAM ([Topaz docs](https://docs.topazlabs.com/topaz-photo/dust-and-scratch)).

## GFPGAN v1.4 is the only face model that is fast, ONNX-ready and permissive

Benchmarks rank face restorers differently from what can actually ship. In the OSDFace paper's CelebA-Test table, one-step diffusion OSDFace leads with FID 17.06 and identity angle 60.07. CodeFormer (62.20), VQFR (62.26) and DAEFR (62.82) follow, and RestoreFormer++ trails at **70.51, the worst identity score in the table** ([OSDFace](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17163v2)). But OSDFace has 978 M parameters and DiffBIR 3,042 M. PGDiff and DifFace need 250–1000 steps. None has an ONNX release, so none is viable on CPU. CodeFormer is technically excellent but is under the **NTU S-Lab License 1.0, non-commercial**, which directs commercial users to "contact the contributor(s)" ([CodeFormer LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sczhou/CodeFormer/master/LICENSE)). GPEN has no licence file, and its README says models were withheld "due to commercial issues" ([GPEN](https://github.com/yangxy/GPEN)). DAEFR states no licence ([DAEFR](https://github.com/LIAGM/DAEFR)), and DifFace is S-Lab ([DifFace](https://github.com/zsyOAOA/DifFace)).

That leaves **GFPGAN v1.4** as the default. Its code is Apache-2.0, and its LICENSE file isolates the NVIDIA StyleGAN2 and DFDNet carve-outs as third-party components ([GFPGAN LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TencentARC/GFPGAN/master/LICENSE)). A secondary model card reports that the v1.4 StyleGAN2 prior was trained from scratch by BasicSR rather than taken from NVIDIA's weights, and that the weights are Apache-2.0 ([mlx-community/GFPGANv1.4-fp32](https://huggingface.co/mlx-community/GFPGANv1.4-fp32)). TencentARC itself says nothing official on commercial use of the weights. The model has 87 M parameters, is about **340 MB in fp32 ONNX** ([facefusion models](https://huggingface.co/facefusion/models-3.0.0/tree/main)), and takes one forward pass per 512² aligned face. No published CPU timing exists. The estimate is **about 1–3 s per face and 0.7–1.5 GB RAM** on a 4–8-core laptop, which is why the face stage is the most urgent item to benchmark on the 5700U. RestoreFormer++ (Apache-2.0, [RestoreFormer++](https://github.com/wzhouxiff/RestoreFormerPlusPlus)) is the fallback, but its weak identity score makes it a poor fit for family photos. The one tiny model worth watching is CFRNet (2026), with about 2 M parameters and about 23 ms INT8 on an embedded NPU. It has no code, weights or licence yet ([arXiv 2606.06850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06850)).

The surrounding pipeline can stay licence-clean. Faces are detected with **YuNet**, which the app already ships under MIT and which outputs the five landmarks the FFHQ-512 template needs. Each face is warped to 512², restored, warped back through the inverse affine and composited with a feathered mask and a blend factor, the same sequence facefusion uses ([facefusion face_enhancer](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facefusion/facefusion/master/facefusion/processors/modules/face_enhancer/core.py)). Avoid facexlib's RetinaFace and parsing weights, which carry their own licences. GFPGAN has no built-in fidelity knob, so **output blending is its only strength control**. facefusion defaults to 80 % enhanced, but for archival portraits a more conservative default is better.

Identity drift is the main way users lose trust. Topaz admits its model reconstructs "plausible features rather than strictly preserving original pixels" ([Topaz developer docs](https://developer.topazlabs.com/image-models/gigapixel/recover-faces)), and its users complain that face recovery "changed the faces too much" ([Topaz community](https://community.topazlabs.com/t/enhancement-improvement-face-recovery-to-use-reference-photos/44942.md)). NTIRE 2026 treats identity as a hard gate, using AdaFace as the checker ([NTIRE 2026 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10532)). SecurePhotos can do the same with models it already has. After restoring a face, compare its **SFace embedding** with the original's. If similarity falls below a threshold, lower the blend automatically or warn "this face may no longer look like the person". Restoration should also run only on faces smaller than about 300 px, since Topaz itself advises against Recover Faces on portraits and warns of a "plastic" look ([Topaz docs](https://docs.topazlabs.com/photo-ai/enhancements/recover-faces)). A later step could use the app's existing face clusters as identity references, as research methods such as FaceMe and RefSTAR do ([Awesome-Face-Restoration](https://github.com/24wenjie-li/Awesome-Face-Restoration)).

## DDColor colourises in about three seconds and beats DeOldify

DDColor is the clear choice. On ImageNet val5k it posts FID 3.92 (large) and 4.38 (tiny), against 6.59 for DeOldify and 8.72 for Zhang's CIC. Its colourfulness deviation is 0.05, against DeOldify's 16.92, which quantifies DeOldify's known brownish, desaturated output ([DDColor paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.11613)). Its code is **Apache-2.0**, and so is every Hugging Face checkpoint card ([HF ddcolor_modelscope](https://huggingface.co/piddnad/ddcolor_modelscope)). The repo was still maintained in January 2026 and ships an official ONNX export script ([DDColor](https://github.com/piddnad/DDColor)). The `modelscope` checkpoint is the photo default. `artistic` is a "Vivid" alternative, but it adds undisclosed private training data, and one repackager excludes it for that reason ([LibreDDColor card](https://huggingface.co/LibreYOLO/LibreDDColorl-restore)). DeOldify (MIT code and weights) was archived in October 2024 ([DeOldify](https://github.com/jantic/DeOldify)). Zhang's Caffe model no longer loads in OpenCV 5, as found during local testing. Newer colourisers are either Stable Diffusion-based, which rules them out on CPU, or non-commercial: CtrlColor (S-Lab), UniColor (CC BY-NC 4.0) and CT2 (CC BY-NC notice) ([CtrlColor](https://github.com/ZhexinLiang/Control-Color), [UniColor](https://github.com/luckyhzt/unicolor)). ChromaGAN is AGPL-3.0 ([ChromaGAN](https://github.com/pvitoria/ChromaGAN)).

Measured on the 5700U, **DDColor-tiny at 256 px takes 0.70 s (0.50 s at 4 threads, ~0.7 GB)**. **DDColor-tiny at 512 px takes 1.54 s (~2.2 GB)**, and **DDColor-large at 512 px takes 3.15 s fp32 / 2.94 s int8 (~2.4–2.9 GB)**. Transferring chroma onto a 12 MP original costs another 0.67 s. One finding should become a hard engineering rule: the **fp16 DDColor-tiny ONNX ran for over 8 minutes without finishing five inferences**. That matches ORT's documentation that "the CPU version of ONNX Runtime doesn't support float16 ops" ([ORT float16](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/model-optimizations/float16.html)). Ship fp32 or int8 on CPU and never fp16. Dynamic int8 barely sped up DDColor-large because convolutions dominate, but it shrank the file from 911 MB to 304 MB. The best ready-made small file is the Apache-2.0 int8 tiny export (112.7 MB, mean ab error about 1.1 vs fp32) ([toolsmonk/ddcolor-tiny](https://huggingface.co/toolsmonk/ddcolor-tiny)). The 456 MB "large" community export has no licence tag, so build large from the official checkpoint rather than use it.

The way colourisation runs matters as much as the model. Take only Lab L from the input, because scanned "B&W" prints often carry a sepia cast that would bias the model. Run at 512 (or 256 in fast mode), upsample the predicted ab channels with full-resolution L as an edge guide, and recombine them with the untouched original L, following the pattern of the reference export ([edgetools/ddcolor](https://huggingface.co/edgetools/ddcolor)). Because the ab output can be cached, an **intensity slider (0–150 % chroma scale) updates instantly with no re-inference**, which matters given that reviewers of Photoshop and Google Photos colourisation complained of over-red lips and "muted" vintage tones respectively ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2021/11/04/how-adobes-neural-filters-make-colorizing-photos-way-easier/), [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2019/10/07/google-photos-colorize-gallery/)).

## Every usable model inherits an unresolved dataset-licence question

The licence picture splits into three tiers. Clean code-and-weights licences with no special training-data concerns cover Real-ESRGAN (BSD-3, [Real-ESRGAN](https://github.com/xinntao/Real-ESRGAN)), the BOPBTL detector (MIT) and the SCUNet/FBCNN/SPAN code (Apache-2.0). LaMa's weights come from Places, whose images "belong to the image owners" ([Places365](https://github.com/CSAILVision/places365)). That is the ordinary scraped-data grey zone. The second tier is where SecurePhotos' most valuable features sit. GFPGAN and RestoreFormer++ are trained on **FFHQ**, whose metadata is **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** and whose images are under mixed Flickr licences "for non-commercial purposes" ([NVlabs/ffhq-dataset](https://github.com/NVlabs/ffhq-dataset)). DDColor and DeOldify are trained on **ImageNet**, whose access terms limit the dataset to "non-commercial research and educational purposes" ([DrivenData forum](https://community.drivendata.org/t/clarification-on-imagenet-pre-trained-weights-under-open-source-commercial-rules/11475)). No court ruling or dataset-owner statement settles whether weights inherit those terms. Practitioners call the question "unsettled in most jurisdictions" ([WCR.LEGAL](https://wcr.legal/fine-tuned-model-license/)), and a Keras issue on exactly this point has stayed open for years ([keras #13362](https://github.com/keras-team/keras/issues/13362)). The third tier is explicitly non-commercial and must be excluded: CodeFormer, DifFace, StableSR and InvSR (S-Lab); SUPIR and HYPIR (SupPixel); MAT, UniColor and CT2 (CC BY-NC); and GPEN (labelled non-commercial). ChromaGAN (AGPL) and JPEGDestroyer (GPL) are copyleft. DiffBIR and PMRF look permissive, but they pull in SwinIR-Face checkpoints from DifFace (S-Lab) ([DiffBIR](https://github.com/XPixelGroup/DiffBIR), [PMRF](https://github.com/ohayonguy/PMRF)).

The steelman for shipping the second tier is industry practice. Apache/MIT-declared weights trained on FFHQ or ImageNet sit inside many commercial products, and Qualcomm's AI Hub redistributes DDColor as Apache-2.0 ([Qualcomm AI Hub](https://aihub.qualcomm.com/models/ddcolor)). The counter-argument is that FFHQ adds a share-alike clause and a request not to use it for facial recognition work, and that "non-commercial" is ambiguous for publicly distributed software. That ambiguity would sharpen immediately if SecurePhotos ever became paid. The pragmatic position for an MIT-licensed free app has four parts. Ship these models as residual risk. Record each model's licence, source URL, training dataset and SHA-256 in a third-party notices file. Never use model authors' names in marketing, which the S-Lab and Apache endorsement clauses forbid. Get a lawyer's view before any paid tier. Downloading the models from upstream on demand, rather than bundling them, arguably shifts redistribution to the upstream host, but that theory is untested.

## On-demand versions, honest labels and an identity gate define the product

Every product researched handles restoration the same way. It runs on demand, writes a new linked version, keeps the original and offers a comparison. MyHeritage says originals "should not be modified or replaced", and it offers a drag slider and a per-face strip that zooms into each enhanced face ([MyHeritage](https://blog.myheritage.com/2020/06/introducing-the-myheritage-photo-enhancer/)). Lightroom Enhance writes a new DNG stacked with the source and refuses to enhance it twice ([Adobe Help](https://helpx.adobe.com/lightroom-cc/using/enhance-details.html)). Photoshop's Photo Restoration exposes separate Photo enhancement, Enhance face and Scratch reduction sliders, with scratch reduction off by default ([CreativePro](https://creativepro.com/using-the-photo-restoration-neural-filter-in-photoshop/)). SecurePhotos should store each result as a child version of the encrypted original, with one-tap revert. It should keep separate toggles for **Repair damage, Clean up, Upscale, Restore faces and Colourise**, so users on slow CPUs pay only for what they need. Face restoration and colourisation must never run automatically during import.

Three UX choices let a CPU-only app still feel fast. First, an **instant preview** runs on a 512 px copy or a single face crop and shows it in the compare slider within seconds, before the user commits to the full render. Second, a **queue panel** offers progress, cancel, ETA and a notification when done. The ETA comes from a first-use calibration that measures milliseconds per tile and multiplies by the tile count. Third, controls that need no re-inference: chroma intensity, face blend and overall strength re-blend cached outputs in real time. The face controls are the most important part. Give a per-face list with on/off toggles and a zoomed before/after, a conservative default blend, an auto-skip for faces over about 300 px, the SFace identity check described above, and disclaimer copy that the faces are "reconstructed estimates". MyHeritage uses this kind of wording ("infers what the original faces may have looked like"), and its own reviewers saw "very disturbing results" on damaged faces ([Techlicious](https://techlicious.com/blog/my-heritage-photo-enhancer-colorizer)). Colourisation should be presented as "one plausible colouring" with a way back to black and white.

Labelling maps cleanly onto the IPTC vocabulary. `algorithmicallyEnhanced` means "modification or correction by algorithm without changing the main content", such as sharpening and noise reduction. `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` means "augmentation, correction or enhancement using a Generative AI model, such as with inpainting or outpainting" ([IPTC DigitalSourceType](https://cv.iptc.org/newscodes/digitalsourcetype/)). Denoise and upscale belong under the first. GFPGAN face reconstruction and DDColor colourisation belong under the second. LaMa damage repair is the edge case. It is texture-level, but IPTC's definition explicitly names inpainting, so the conservative choice is to tag repaired versions as `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` too. Exports should also record the original medium (`print`) when known. Inside the app, a small wand or palette badge on the version, following MyHeritage, makes the label visible. This matters legally now. **EU AI Act Article 50 obligations have applied since 2 Aug 2026**. Article 50(2) requires providers of systems that generate or manipulate images to mark outputs in machine-readable form, exempting only systems with "an assistive function for standard editing" or that "do not substantially alter the input data" ([Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content)). "Substantially" is undefined ([Search Engine Journal](https://searchenginejournal.com/eu-commission-article-50-ai-transparency-law-has-four-exemptions/584528)), but inventing a face or adding colour is hard to call standard editing. The 2 Dec 2026 grace period covers only systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026, so a feature launched now should mark outputs from day one. Article 50(4) deepfake disclosure falls mainly on the deployer, meaning the user who publishes the image, and the test is objective, so whether the subject is plausibly real is what counts. Visible badges make that duty easy for users to meet. The personal-use and open-source exclusions were not checked against the regulation text. IPTC XMP is the cheap, widely read baseline. C2PA can follow later, but an individual developer's self-signed manifest will not show as trusted.

## Deployment: CPU EP first, fp32 or calibrated int8, tiled, downloaded on demand

The engine should treat **CPUExecutionProvider as the primary path**. Leave `intra_op_num_threads` at the default (one per physical core) or cap it at cores − 1 while the user is active. Disable spinning (`session.intra_op.allow_spinning = 0`) to save power, keep `ORT_SEQUENTIAL` and let the OS handle affinity, as ORT recommends ([ORT threading](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/tune-performance/threading.html)). The local measurements add a twist. On the 16-thread 5700U, **4 threads were faster than the default** for DDColor-tiny-256 and the int8 models, so a one-time per-model micro-benchmark that picks the thread count is worth building. Int8 has to be earned. ORT says static QDQ suits CNNs, that "S8S8 with QOperator will be slow on x86-64", and that old CPUs without VNNI often run int8 *slower* ([ORT quantization](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/model-optimizations/quantization.html)). The 5700U is Zen 2, which has no VNNI, and that fits int8 tiny-512 being slower than fp32 at 16 threads (2.82 s vs 1.54 s). Ship fp32 as the reference and offer per-channel static int8 only for models that pass a visual and SFace/PSNR check. Choose between them at runtime from a CPU feature check. GAN decoders like GFPGAN are especially sensitive to int8.

Memory needs the same discipline. Tile super-resolution at 128–256 px with 16–32 px overlap and feathered seams. That bounds RAM, keeps shapes static and gives natural progress ticks and cancellation points. Process tiles sequentially, load one model at a time and free sessions after each job, and offer to downscale inputs over about 12 MP. SCUNet's 5.7 GB peak needs smaller tiles and the CPU memory arena turned off before it is safe to offer on 8 GB machines. Run inference in a separate worker process at below-normal priority with EcoQoS, which Microsoft warns against only for foreground, performance-critical work ([Microsoft Learn QoS](https://github.com/MicrosoftDocs/win32/blob/docs/desktop-src/ProcThread/quality-of-service.md)), so that cancel can kill it outright.

Models should be optional downloads. The installer keeps general-x4v3 (already shipped) and YuNet/SFace. Everything else (detector 151 MB, LaMa 208 MB, GFPGAN 340 MB, DDColor 113–911 MB, SCUNet 74 MB, FBCNN 288 MB) downloads on first use from pinned upstream URLs. Each file is checked against a **SHA-256 in a manifest shipped and signed with the app** before it is ever loaded. Users who want strict offline operation can import model files manually or install an "offline bundle". Re-export GFPGAN and DDColor-large from the official checkpoints rather than redistributing facefusion's or anonymous community files, and parity-check any third-party ONNX against PyTorch. **DirectML is an optional bonus only.** It is in "maintenance mode" / "sustained engineering", and new work has moved to Windows ML ([microsoft/DirectML](https://github.com/microsoft/DirectML), [ORT DirectML EP](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/execution-providers/DirectML-ExecutionProvider.html)). It can be slower than CPU on weak iGPUs ([DirectML issue 175](https://summer-heart-0930.chufeiyun1688.workers.dev/https/github.com/microsoft/DirectML/issues/175)), and it has produced black output on an AMD iGPU with Real-ESRGAN ([Magpie #772](https://www.github.com/Blinue/Magpie/issues/772)). If it is offered, export at opset ≤ 20, avoid DeformConv, run a smoke test, and use it only when it beats CPU on that machine.

## Conclusion

The core finding is that the CPU constraint and the licensing constraint point to the same models. The models fast enough for a Ryzen laptop are compact feed-forward CNNs. Among those, only GFPGAN, DDColor, Real-ESRGAN, LaMa and the BOPBTL detector carry permissive code and weight licences. The models that win benchmarks are diffusion models that are both too heavy and mostly non-commercial. So SecurePhotos gives up little real quality by staying on CPU. What it cannot get from benchmarks is evidence that these models behave on *its* inputs: sepia-cast, grainy, halftoned and creased scans. No published benchmark covers that domain, and quality was never judged in the local tests. The next steps, in order, are as follows. Measure GFPGAN per-face CPU time and RAM on the 5700U, since it is the only stage still purely estimated. Then build a 20–30-image set of real family scans and A/B test DDColor tiny vs large, 256 vs 512 and int8 vs fp32, GFPGAN blend levels against SFace similarity, and general-x4v3 against a SPAN candidate. Then test whether feeding face and colour models luminance-only input avoids colour casts on monochrome prints. That behaviour is assumed in this report but untested.

The second takeaway is that labelling and the identity gate are features in their own right, not just compliance work. An app that promises privacy and fidelity for family archives can make "we tell you what the AI changed, and we check it is still grandma" its selling point, where cloud enhancers offer one-tap magic. The FFHQ and ImageNet question will stay open. A free MIT app can live with it if it documents provenance carefully. Moving to a paid tier is the point at which a lawyer's opinion, or a face model trained on consented data, stops being optional.
